Your team already knows how to run a sprint. This course migrates you to something newer: AI-DLC, the AI-Driven Development Lifecycle — a methodology where AI drives the routine work of planning and building, and humans supervise, decide, and approve. Not AI sprinkled onto your old process. A process redesigned around AI.

AI-DLC comes from AWS (introduced in mid-2025, with its workflow rules later open-sourced as awslabs/aidlc-workflows). Its starting observation is blunt: most teams bolted an assistant onto an unchanged lifecycle — a developer asks for snippets inside the same two-week sprint — and got modest gains. AI-DLC inverts the relationship. The AI initiates: it elaborates your intent into a plan, asks clarifying questions, proposes designs, writes code and tests. Humans do what only humans can: supply business context, resolve ambiguity, and validate at every checkpoint. The mantra is AI proposes, humans approve.

The vocabulary shift

AI-DLC renames the familiar pieces deliberately, to signal that the economics of planning have changed:

  • Bolt replaces the sprint — a short, intense cycle measured in hours or days, in which the team validates an AI-proposed plan, the AI executes it, and the results are reviewed.
  • Unit of Work replaces the epic — a chunk of intent sized so a bolt (or a few) can deliver it.
  • Mob Elaboration — an Inception ritual: the AI turns a business intent into requirements and clarifying questions, and the whole team answers them together, in one room, in real time.
  • Mob Construction — the same collaboration during building: the AI proposes architecture, design, and code; the team resolves the technical decisions on the spot.
  • Persistent context — the decisions, conventions, and project knowledge live in the repository (not in someone's chat history), so every session and every teammate's AI starts informed. You'll build exactly this in the Persistent Context and AI-engineering modules.

Three phases, one loop

The lifecycle keeps a recognizable shape — Inception → Construction → Operations — but inside each phase runs the same loop: AI proposes → humans validate → AI executes → humans review. Inception turns intent into validated units of work (Mob Elaboration lives here). Construction turns units into designed, coded, tested software (Mob Construction lives here). Operations deploys and runs it. What never changes across all three: a human is accountable at every checkpoint. AI-DLC is as much a governance model as a speed model.

The one reframe

In a sprint, you were the author of every line, and process existed to coordinate humans. In AI-DLC you are the supervisor and validator, and process exists to keep fast-moving AI work safe and aligned. That is the whole shift. Everything else in this course is practice.

Honest expectations

AWS and its partners report dramatic gains from AI-DLC engagements — vendor-reported 10–15x, including compressing months of planned work into days of mob sessions — versus the ~10–15% typically measured from unstructured assistant adoption. The gap, in AWS's telling, is the methodology. Treat those numbers as vendor benchmarks: directionally interesting, unverified in your context. And know the real trade-off going in: bolts are faster in calendar time but hungry for senior attention — validation checkpoints with the whole team are the new constraint, and this course treats reviewing well as a first-class skill.

Honest from the start

Your AI collaborator is sometimes confidently wrong — it will invent a method that doesn't exist and describe it convincingly. AI-DLC's answer isn't to trust less or more; it's structural: every artifact passes a human checkpoint before it proceeds. Trust nothing you didn't validate.

Most teams don't start with an AI-ready repo — they have an existing codebase and want AI to be useful on it. The good news: you don't rewrite anything. You add a thin layer of context and tooling that turns a generic assistant into one that already knows your project.

This is the retrofit. It's the same move as onboarding a human teammate — you write down how the project works, point out the sharp edges, and give them the tools to help — except here the "teammate" reads those files on every task. We'll use our own Prior Auth service as the worked example, because it has already been through this transformation.

What "AI-ready" actually means

An AI-ready repo carries five things, in roughly this order of value:

  • Shared context — a CLAUDE.md and a .github/copilot-instructions.md that describe the app, the stack, and the conventions, so you stop repeating yourself in every prompt.
  • Encoded conventions — Skills that package a repeated workflow or rule (like our scoring conventions) and load automatically when relevant.
  • Specialized helpers — subagents for focused jobs such as code review or security review, each with its own prompt and tools.
  • Automation — PR review and CI hooks so the assistant helps on every pull request, not just at your desk.
  • Guardrails — a real test suite. AI moves fast; tests are what let you trust the result. A repo with good tests is far safer to hand to an assistant.
Highest-leverage first move

If you do only one thing, write a good CLAUDE.md. Context beats cleverness: the same assistant produces dramatically better work once it knows your layering, your "don't do this," and how to build and test. Everything else compounds on top of it.

The retrofit, step by step

  1. Write the context files. Don't start from a blank page: launch claude in the repo and run /init — Claude Code scans the codebase and drafts a CLAUDE.md at the root for you. Then edit the draft to add what a scan can't see: the conventions (and anti-conventions), the known gotchas, and exactly how to build and test. Mirror the essentials into .github/copilot-instructions.md (Copilot Chat in VS Code has a Generate instructions action that drafts this file the same way). Commit both — they're shared team memory.
  2. Make sure there are tests, and say how to run them. If coverage is thin, this is the moment to add it (AI can help — see the testing lab). Tests are your safety net for everything that follows.
  3. Capture your first repeated workflow as a Skill. The fifth time you paste the same release checklist or rules explanation, move it into a .claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md with a sharp description so it triggers on the right tasks.
  4. Add a review subagent. A .claude/agents/code-reviewer.md gives you a consistent first-pass reviewer that keeps the verbose diff-reading out of your main session.
  5. Automate on pull requests. Run claude /install-github-app to add PR review to CI, so every change gets a first look automatically.
  6. Write it down and roll it out. A short "how we use AI here" note in the README, and a light convention for keeping the context files current, turns a personal setup into a team capability.

The fifth time you paste the same conventions or the same checklist into a prompt, stop. That repetition is a Skill waiting to be written. A Skill teaches Claude something once, and it loads that knowledge on its own whenever a task calls for it.

You already met one in the last track: the service repo ships .claude/skills/prior-auth-rules/SKILL.md, which encodes how the auto-approval scoring works. What a Skill is

A Skill is a folder containing a SKILL.md file: YAML frontmatter on top, Markdown instructions below. It lives in one of two places, and the folder name is the Skill's name:

  • .claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md — project scope, committed to the repo and shared with the team.
  • ~/.claude/skills/<name>/SKILL.md — personal scope, available in all your projects.
the frontmatter of the Skill shipped in this repo
---
name: prior-auth-rules
description: Conventions for the Prior Auth auto-approval rules engine. Use
  whenever adding, changing, or reviewing approval scoring logic, the
  auto-approval threshold, or AutoApprovalService — so scoring stays
  explainable, deterministic, and covered by tests.
---

# Prior Auth rules engine
Apply this whenever the task touches auto-approval scoring...

The one thing that makes a Skill work

Skills are model-invoked. You don't type a command to run one — Claude reads the description and decides, on its own, whether the current task matches. That makes the description the most important line in the file. Name the trigger conditions plainly: "Use whenever adding, changing, or reviewing approval scoring" fires reliably; a vague "rules helper" does not.

Skill vs CLAUDE.md vs slash command

Three tools, three jobs. CLAUDE.md is always-on context — facts and conventions Claude sees every time. A Skill is on-demand expertise — a procedure or body of knowledge loaded only when relevant, so it costs almost nothing the rest of the time. A slash command is an explicit action you trigger by typing /name. Reach for a Skill when a fact in CLAUDE.md has grown into a multi-step procedure you keep repeating.

Anatomy and growth

Only SKILL.md is required — that alone is a working Skill. As it grows, a Skill folder can hold optional resources that Claude pulls in as needed:

  • scripts/ — helper scripts for deterministic steps.
  • references/ — longer detail Claude loads on demand. Keep SKILL.md focused (aim under ~500 lines) and move depth here.
  • assets/ — templates, examples, or files the Skill produces.
Write once, use everywhere

Skills follow an open standard, so the same SKILL.md works in Claude Code, the Claude apps, the API, and other compatible agents. And the two tools have converged: a .claude/commands/deploy.md and a .claude/skills/deploy/SKILL.md both create /deploy — a slash command is now a Skill with no extra features.

A subagent is a second Claude with its own context window, its own tools, and its own instructions — a specialist you delegate a focused job to. The verbose part of the work stays in the subagent's context, and only the summary comes back to your main conversation.

Why they exist

Your context window is finite. Read thirty files or a giant test log into your main thread and the quality of everything after it degrades. A subagent solves this: it does the heavy reading in its own context and hands back just the result. Think of subagents as browser tabs for a Claude Code session — a place to chase a tangent without losing the main thread.

Where they live and what's in them

A subagent is a Markdown file: YAML frontmatter, then the system prompt as the body.

  • .claude/agents/<name>.md — project scope, committed and shared with the team.
  • ~/.claude/agents/<name>.md — personal scope, available in every project.
the code-reviewer shipped in this repo (frontmatter verbatim; body abridged)
---
name: code-reviewer
description: Reviews recent code changes in the Prior Auth service for
  quality, correctness, and adherence to this repo's conventions. Use
  proactively after implementing a change or before opening a pull
  request. Invoke explicitly with "have the code-reviewer subagent
  review my changes".
tools: Read, Grep, Glob, Bash
model: sonnet
---
You are a senior code reviewer for the Prior Auth Portal service…
(…the full ~60-line prompt walks the review: diff, changed files,
CLAUDE.md conventions, findings grouped by severity with file:line)

Four fields carry the weight:

  • description drives delegation — Claude reads it to decide when to hand a task off. Specific, action-oriented phrasing ("use proactively before commits touching auth") makes it delegate more readily.
  • tools is least-privilege. List only what the job needs — the shipped reviewer gets Read, Grep, Glob plus Bash (needed to run git diff) and no edit tools — though Bash could technically touch files, so it's the prompt's "you do not modify code" rule that truly forbids fixes (the next module returns to this). Omit the field and it inherits every tool.
  • model pins sonnet, opus, or haiku, or says inherit to match your main session.
  • Location sets scope, as above.

Creating and using one

Two ways to make a subagent. Ask Claude — "create a subagent that reviews Java changes against CLAUDE.md, read-only" — and it writes the .claude/agents/<name>.md file for you to refine. Or write the file by hand. The /agents command is the manager for listing and editing them.

To use one, Claude delegates automatically when a task matches the description, or you invoke it explicitly:

explicit invocation in a Claude Code session
> use the code-reviewer subagent to review my staged changes
Built-ins & parallelism

Claude Code already ships subagents like Explore and Plan (and a general-purpose worker) — get comfortable with those before writing custom ones. And multiple subagents can run at once: "research the auth, database, and API modules in parallel using separate subagents" keeps three independent investigations off your main thread.

The one gotcha

Subagent files are loaded at session start. If you create or edit a .claude/agents/*.md file while Claude Code is running, restart the session to pick it up. (Agents created or edited through /agents take effect immediately — that asymmetry catches everyone once.)

MCP — reaching outside the repo

Everything so far has worked with files in your project. MCP (the Model Context Protocol) lets Claude Code connect to external tools and data through a standard interface — an issue tracker, a database, internal docs, a browser. You add a server, and Claude can use it as part of a task.

adding an MCP server
$ claude mcp add <name> -- <command>  # register a server (see claude mcp add --help);
# or commit a .mcp.json so the whole team gets it

Now a task like "pull the acceptance criteria from ticket PA-142 and implement it" can read the ticket directly, instead of you copying it in. Reach for MCP when the context or the action you need lives outside the codebase.

Hooks — deterministic enforcement

Skills, subagents, and CLAUDE.md all influence the model — but the model still decides. Hooks are different: they run your code, every time, regardless of what the model chooses. A hook is a shell command Claude Code runs automatically at a lifecycle event, declared in .claude/settings.json (project, shared) or ~/.claude/settings.json (personal).

The events you'll use most:

  • PreToolUse — runs before a tool call and can block it (exit code 2). Good for guardrails: refuse edits outside src/, or block reading a secrets file.
  • PostToolUse — runs after a tool call. Can't undo it, but perfect for auto-formatting after every file write, or logging for an audit trail.
  • Stop and SessionStart — react when Claude finishes, or inject context (e.g. recent commits) at the start of a session.
.claude/settings.json — auto-format on every edit
{
  "hooks": {
    "PostToolUse": [
      {
        "matcher": "Write|Edit",
        "hooks": [
          { "type": "command", "command": "your-formatter-here" }
        ]
      }
    ]
  }
}

For this Java service that command might run your formatter or a quick ./mvnw -q compile; the matcher is the tool name to intercept.

Hooks run as code

A hook executes with your permissions, on your machine, automatically. Treat hook scripts as real code: keep them small, review them, keep secrets out of the script body, and remember a failing hook can interrupt the workflow. The exact event list and JSON schema move quickly — check the hooks reference in the docs before relying on a specific field.

Before you hand real work to an AI collaborator, it pays to know what it actually is. Not magic, not a junior developer — a very capable prediction engine with sharp strengths and predictable failure modes. AI-DLC's checkpoints exist precisely because of how this thing thinks.

What it's genuinely good at

A large language model has read an enormous amount of code and prose. Give it clear intent and enough context and it will draft plans, ask sensible clarifying questions, write idiomatic code, generate test cases, and explain unfamiliar codebases — in seconds, tirelessly, in any framework your team uses. That's why AI-DLC lets it drive the routine work: elaboration, drafting, construction.

The three limits that shape the methodology

  • It only knows what's in the conversation. The model has no privileged view of your repo, your standards, or your last decision — only what's loaded into its context. Feed it nothing, and it fills the gaps with plausible guesses.
  • It predicts; it doesn't verify. Output is what's likely, not what's checked. That's why it can invent a method that doesn't exist and describe it fluently — the classic confident error.
  • It forgets between sessions. Close the session and the shared understanding evaporates — unless you persisted it. This is exactly why AI-DLC makes persistent context in the repository a first-class concept (Module 7).
Why AI-DLC is shaped the way it is

Each ritual answers one limit. Mob Elaboration exists because the model needs context and surfaces its gaps as questions. Validation checkpoints exist because prediction isn't verification. Persistent context exists because sessions forget. The methodology is the model's limits, engineered around.

Claude Code is a terminal-based agent. You give it intent in plain language; it reads your repo, proposes a plan, edits files, runs commands and tests, and works through multi-step tasks. It reads CLAUDE.md automatically — which makes it a natural fit for AI-DLC's persistent context — and it's the tool this very course is authored with.

GitHub Copilot lives in your editor and on GitHub.com. It completes code as you type (ghost text you accept with Tab), answers questions in Copilot Chat with @workspace context, reviews pull requests, and reads repo-wide instructions from .github/copilot-instructions.md.

Where each shines in a bolt

  • Elaboration & multi-file construction: Claude Code's territory — it can take a unit of work from intent to plan to edited, tested code across the codebase.
  • In-flow completion & quick questions: Copilot's territory — when a human is typing (reviewing, adjusting, exploring), ghost text and chat keep them moving.
  • Pull-request review: both — Claude via the GitHub app/Action, Copilot via its built-in reviewer. You'll wire up both in the automation track.
Why every lab is dual-path

Running the same bolt through both tools teaches you the methodology rather than a vendor's workflow. Teams that learn AI-DLC tool-agnostically can switch engines — Q, Kiro, Claude, Copilot — without relearning how they work.

Honest comparison rule

This course won't tell you one tool "wins." Most migrating teams end up using both — and the labs are designed so you feel why for yourself.

Every bolt in this course runs on one real codebase: the Prior Auth Portal, a small Spring Boot service. A provider submits a prior authorization request for a member; the service scores it and either auto-approves it or routes it to a human reviewer. Ten minutes here saves confusion in every lab that follows.

The request's journey

The layers, and why they're strict

  • Web (src/main/java/com/portal/priorauth/web/PriorAuthController.java + DTO records — DTOs, the data-transfer objects that define what crosses the wire) — validates input, maps to AuthResponse, never touches the database. Errors go through GlobalExceptionHandler.
  • Service (src/main/java/com/portal/priorauth/service/PriorAuthService.java) — orchestrates: create, score, persist, and the reviewer's decide flow.
  • Rules engine (src/main/java/com/portal/priorauth/service/AutoApprovalService.java) — pure and deterministic: base 0.90, minus penalties (review-required service −0.20, units > 10 −0.15, blank diagnosis −0.10), clamped, compared to AUTO_APPROVE_THRESHOLD = 0.85.
  • Domain (src/main/java/com/portal/priorauth/domain/PriorAuthRequest.java) — the entity mutates only through applyDecision(...); the lifecycle is SUBMITTED → APPROVED | PENDING_REVIEW → (reviewer) APPROVED | DENIED.

These boundaries aren't ceremony — they're what makes AI work reviewable. When every bolt's output must fit a known shape, validation checkpoints are fast.

As a provider, when my request is denied, I can file one appeal with a reason, so that the request gets a second human review instead of being a dead end.

Elaboration — the Mob Elaboration beat

Don't jump to code. Hand the intent to the AI and ask it to elaborate:

elaboration prompt (either tool)
Here is a feature intent for this service: "A provider can file one

A good elaboration comes back with a plan and questions like these — and answering them together, on the record, is the ritual. Here are the questions a strong elaboration surfaces, with the answers this team decided:

  • "Should an appeal introduce a new status, or reuse PENDING_REVIEW?" — Reuse PENDING_REVIEW. The reviewer flow already exists; keep the enum stable this bolt.
  • "How do we enforce 'one appeal only'?" — Add an appealedAt timestamp to com/portal/priorauth/domain/PriorAuthRequest.java; a second appeal is rejected with 409.

  • "Is the reason required, and where is it stored?" — Required (validated, max 500 chars), recorded via the existing decisionReason on the transition.
Persist the decisions

Four construction bolts in, you've noticed where AI-DLC actually spends human effort: the checkpoints. Validation is the load-bearing wall of the whole methodology — so it deserves its own craft. This module is that craft, for the two artifacts you validate most: proposed plans and produced code.

Checkpoint one: the plan, before construction

Cheapest place to catch problems. Before approving any AI-proposed plan, ask four questions:

  • Scope — does the file list match the intent? A plan that touches ten files for a one-endpoint feature is telling you something.
  • Invariants — does anything brush a business rule (here: the 0.85 threshold, the status lifecycle)? Those changes need explicit mob sign-off, never incidental inclusion.
  • Assumptions — what did the AI decide that nobody actually answered? Unasked clarifying questions resurface here as silent assumptions.
  • Verifiability — how will this be proven? A plan without a test-and-run story isn't a plan yet.

Checkpoint two: the code, before it merges

Reviewing AI code is reviewing a fast, well-read author with no stake in being right. Priorities, in order:

  • Correctness against the criteria — recompute one expected value by hand; generated tests can encode generated mistakes.
  • Invariants and conventions — state changes via guarded methods, entities never over the wire, layering intact.
  • Invented APIs — any method you don't recognize gets an open-the-source check; this is where confident errors hide.
  • The tests test something — would they fail if the behavior broke? Module 11's mutation trick works everywhere.
The asymmetry to internalize

AI produces in minutes what takes an hour to validate well — so validation attention is the scarce resource in AI-DLC, and everything in this course that reduces its cost compounds: tight persistent context (fewer convention errors to catch), strong suites (mechanical proof instead of squinting), and small bolts (small diffs review honestly; nobody reviews 2,000 lines well).

Validation in both directions

The review lens points both ways. Before requesting a teammate's checkpoint, run the AI over your own change — "review this diff against CLAUDE.md; list problems by severity" — and arrive pre-reviewed. In the automation track you'll make this structural: a review subagent with your standards baked in, and PR-time review that runs on every pull request automatically.

The failure mode to name out loud

Checkpoint fatigue. When every artifact arrives plausible and well-formatted, approval drifts toward a reflex — and a rubber-stamp checkpoint is worse than none, because it launders unreviewed work as reviewed. The countermeasures are structural, not moral: keep bolts small, rotate who validates, and require the validator to run something, not just read it.

You've run the elaboration beat inside single bolts. Mob Elaboration is that beat at full scale: AI-DLC's Inception ritual, where a business intent becomes validated requirements and right-sized units of work — with the whole cross-functional team in the room, answering the AI's questions together.

Why a mob, not a document

Traditional inception serializes: the PM writes, the analyst refines, engineering estimates, questions bounce through tickets for weeks. Mob Elaboration collapses that into hours because the AI drafts instantly and the people who hold the answers — product, engineering, QA, sometimes compliance — are present to give them as the questions surface. The AI's clarifying questions become the meeting agenda; ambiguity gets resolved at conversation speed instead of ticket speed. And the questions are often ones the team hadn't settled among themselves — the ritual surfaces internal disagreement early, which is half its value.

The mechanics of a session

  • Input: a written intent, a sentence or a paragraph ("providers need a self-service appeals flow with an audit trail").
  • Round 1 — elaborate: the AI proposes requirements and asks its questions. A facilitator drives; the mob answers aloud; a scribe (or the AI itself) records each ruling.
  • Round 2 — decompose: the AI proposes units of work from the validated requirements. The team resizes: each unit should be deliverable in a bolt or a few — "appeal submission endpoint" is a unit; "rebuild the portal" is not.
  • Output: validated requirements, sized units, and — critically — every ruling persisted to the repo (CLAUDE.md or a decisions file). Inception's product isn't a backlog; it's context.
The sizing test for a unit of work

Can the team state its acceptance criteria in one screen, and can a bolt deliver it with a real validation checkpoint at the end? If criteria sprawl, split the unit. If a unit is all judgment and no construction, it's a decision to make in the mob, not a unit to schedule.

The honest cost

Mob sessions concentrate senior attention: four people for an afternoon is real money, and it's the price of the speed. Budget it deliberately — crisp facilitation, timeboxed rounds, decisions recorded once — rather than discovering it in calendar pain. Faster in calendar time, yes; cheaper in senior attention, no.

Data that never enters a prompt or a context file

  • Secrets: credentials, tokens, connection strings. Not in prompts, not in CLAUDE.md, not in workflow YAML — CI secrets live in the secret store — the PR-automation lab (Module 26) sets this up.

Work that stays human-led

AI-DLC says AI proposes and humans approve — but some proposals shouldn't be delegated to a first draft at all:

  • Security-sensitive code (authentication/authorization, crypto, session handling): AI may review and explain; a human writes the implementation, or owns it line by line.
  • The business invariants themselves: the 0.85 threshold is a clinical-financial policy decision. AI can implement a change to it; only the accountable humans can decide it.
  • Anything you couldn't explain to an auditor: in a regulated domain, "the AI generated it and tests passed" is not a control. Every merged change needs a human who can narrate what it does and why.

Containing confident errors

You've met hallucinations in the bolts; at team scale the containment is structural: invented APIs die at compile time (one reason typed, compiled Java is friendly ground for AI work); invented behavior dies at the test checkpoint; invented facts about your domain die only if persistent context states the truth — which is why recording rulings isn't housekeeping, it's a safety control.

The team's one-line policy

What happens to estimation

Story points mostly encoded implementation effort: how much code, how gnarly. The two-week container assumed work needed two weeks. What independent research actually shows

Come to leadership already knowing the literature — it's two-sided, and knowing both sides is what makes you credible:

  • Adoption and perception are sky-high.   • Perception is not measurement. Intent

A reviewer reports that some large physical-therapy requests seem to auto-approve when policy says they should be scrutinized. As the developer on triage, produce a defect ticket good enough that any teammate could pick it up cold: analyzed, reproduced, and precisely scoped.

The three moves, with AI in each

  • Analyze. Feed the AI the symptom, not a conclusion, and ask for ranked hypotheses against the actual code. Vague reports become searchable: "large requests auto-approving" plus the rules engine source is enough for a good tool to spot a boundary condition worth checking.
  • Reproduce. An unreproduced defect is an opinion. The strongest reproduction in a tested codebase is a failing test — small, deterministic, and it doubles as the fix's finish line. A curl sequence is the acceptable fallback for behavior tests can't reach. Expect a twist here: an obviously large request (the seeded 24-unit one, say) reproduces nothing — it routes to review correctly. A first attempt that "fails to reproduce" is itself a clue: the report said some large requests, so probe the policy's edge, not its middle.
  • Raise. Have the AI draft the ticket from the evidence — then validate every sentence. You're accountable for what's filed; an AI-drafted ticket with an unverified claim is worse than a thin one, because it looks authoritative.

Validated acceptance criteria

Triage is a diagnostic bolt: the intent above is unambiguous, so the team validated these criteria up front. The full elaboration beat — AI clarifying questions and all — returns in the construction bolts (Modules 10 and 12).

  1. A ranked hypothesis list grounded in the real source, with the top hypothesis named to a specific line.
  2. A minimal reproduction: a failing test (or exact curl sequence) demonstrating observed vs. expected.
  3. A ticket containing: title, observed vs. expected behavior, reproduction, suspected location, severity with a reason, and environment/version.
  4. Every factual claim in the ticket personally verified before filing.

Three frameworks dominate how engineering productivity is judged today: DORA's four keys, SPACE, and DORA's AI Capabilities Model. This module is the scoreboard tour.

DORA's four keys: speed and stability, always together

A decade of DORA research settled on four measures of software delivery, deliberately paired so neither half can be gamed at the other's expense:

  • Deployment frequency — how often you ship to production. Lead time for changes — commit to running in production. Together: speed.
  • Change failure rate — the share of deployments causing degraded service. Failed deployment recovery time — how fast you restore. Together: stability.

The pairing is the whole point. A team that doubles deployment frequency while change failure rate climbs hasn't improved — it has moved its problem downstream, where it's more expensive. Any AI-productivity story told in DORA terms must report all four, and the 2025 research says exactly where the risk sits: AI adoption now correlates with higher throughput, but instability rises where foundations aren't solid.

SPACE: productivity is five-dimensional

SPACE (from Forsgren, Storey, and colleagues) exists because single-number "developer productivity" is a trap. Its five dimensions: Satisfaction & well-being, Performance (outcomes, not output), Activity (raw counts — commits, PRs), Communication & collaboration, and Efficiency & flow. Two of its rules matter most for an AI rollout:

  • Never read one dimension alone — measure at least three, mixing system data with perception data (surveys are first-class instruments here, not soft extras).
  • Activity is the trap dimension. AI inflates commits, PRs, and lines of code effortlessly, which is precisely why they must never be targets — only diagnostic denominators. A leader who celebrates "40% more PRs since Copilot" is measuring the tool's exhaust, not the team's delivery.

The AI Capabilities Model: the levers behind the numbers

DORA's 2025 companion model answers the question every leader asks — how do we make sure the AI investment pays? — with seven researched capabilities that amplify AI's benefits: a clear and communicated AI stance, healthy data ecosystems, AI-accessible internal data, strong version control practices, working in small batches, a user-centric focus, and quality internal platforms. Its framing thesis is the one you already know: AI is an amplifier — the same tools magnify strong organizations and struggling ones alike. The model also names value stream management as the force multiplier that keeps local coding-speed gains from evaporating into downstream chaos.

The four keys, one at a time

Lead time & deployment frequency improve semi-naturally with AI — on one condition: batch size stays small. The classic failure: AI generates large, plausible PRs; they pile up at human review; "coding time" shrinks while lead time grows. The bottleneck doesn't disappear — it relocates to validation. Countermeasures, all of which you already practice: bolt-sized units of work, automated first-pass review on every PR (Module 26) so a human is never the first filter, and PR pickup time (from a PR opening to a reviewer's first action on it) as your early-warning gauge — it's the first number to move when review is drowning.

Change failure rate (CFR) & recovery time are the endangered pair — throughput up, stability at risk, per the 2025 findings. These you defend deliberately: the test suite as a merge gate (Module 11's suite isn't pedagogy, it's your CFR defense), named-human validation checkpoints (Module 14), rollback fluency so recovery is a reflex, and escaped-defect tagging so instability surfaces in week two, not quarter three.

SPACE, dimension by dimension

  • Satisfaction: quarterly survey, baselined before rollout. Watch the AI-specific drain — validation fatigue. If reviewers report drowning, throughput is borrowing against burnout.
  • Performance: outcomes only — escape rate, rework share, revert rate. This is where "faster" is tested against "better."
  • Activity: refuse it as a target, report it only as a denominator. Goodhart's law — when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — is not optional here: any activity number you reward, AI will manufacture.
  • Communication & collaboration: review turnaround and knowledge distribution. Mob Elaboration and persistent context genuinely move this — decisions stop living in one head. Bus factor — how many people would have to vanish before the knowledge is lost — is a workable proxy.
  • Efficiency & flow: measure where the waiting moved. AI shrinks writing time and grows queue time at checkpoints; end-to-end flow only improves if validation keeps pace — which is why validation attention is a first-class metric in this course.
